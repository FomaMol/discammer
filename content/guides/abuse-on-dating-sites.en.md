The messages arrive every morning before you are properly awake, warm and a little funny, and over a few weeks they become part of your day. Then comes a request for money, and it does not feel like a request from a stranger. That is how a romance scam is built: the closeness does the asking. A profile, a photo or a long run of affectionate messages shows what someone chose to display. It cannot tell you who is typing.

## Patterns that keep turning up

1. **A quick move off the platform.** Early on, they suggest a private messenger, text messages or email, before you have had a chance to try the dating service’s own safety tools.
2. **A reason they can never meet.** Work, travel, military service, an emergency or plain distance always rules out a video call or a meeting in person.
3. **A crisis with a price tag.** Once trust is in place, trouble arrives, and it has a cost: a ticket, a medical bill, a visa, a delivery, a debt, or an investment opportunity.
4. **A payment that is hard to take back.** Gift-card codes, wire transfers, payment apps, cash and cryptocurrency all make recovery difficult.
5. **Pressure to keep the relationship secret.** If friends and family are kept out of it, nobody is left to give a second opinion.

## An invented story, not a real case

You match with someone who says they are working abroad for a while. They write warmly every day and always have a reason to skip the video call. Then, one evening, they need money for an urgent travel problem. You do not have to decide who they really are from a single odd detail. Pause, send nothing, and look for evidence that does not come from them.

## Slow it down, then check

1. **Send no money or gifts** to an online love interest you have not met in person.
2. **Stay on the platform** until you trust the account and have tried its safety features.
3. **Check independently.** Run a reverse image search on the profile photo, and search the job or story they describe together with the word “scam.” Names that do not match and stories that repeat are warning signs, though neither proves anything alone.
4. **Ask someone you trust.** A friend can spot pressure and gaps in the story that are hard to see when your feelings are involved.
5. **Report the account** to the dating service if it asks for money or looks deceptive.

If you have already sent money or payment details, call your bank or payment provider promptly, and keep the messages and the transaction record for a report to the relevant local authority. There is no shame in making that call.
