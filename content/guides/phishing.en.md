The message arrives over breakfast: your account has a problem, and one button will fix it.

Phishing is social engineering. A message poses as someone you trust so that you will click, open, sign in, pay or hand over information, and it can reach you by email, text, social media, phone call or a chat in a work tool. Nobody has to break in. The trick is to get you to open the door yourself.

## Five things that should slow you down

1. **Urgency or fear you did not expect.** A supposed account problem, invoice, delivery or security alert pushes you to act before you check.
2. **A link that does not say where it goes.** A shortened, misspelled or lookalike domain can lead to a fake sign-in page.
3. **An attachment you were not expecting.** Do not open it just because the sender's name looks familiar.
4. **A request for a password, one-time code or payment details.** A real organization can be reached through a channel you find on your own.
5. **A small mismatch.** An odd sender address, a different reply-to address or a strange request is reason enough to check.

## An invented example: the delivery alert

An alert says an address must be confirmed today for a delivery. The button opens a page that looks like the delivery company's and asks you to sign in. Skip the button. Open a new browser tab and type the company's address yourself, or use the app you already have installed. If the alert is not there, do not enter your details.

## A routine for checking

1. Pause before you click a link, open an attachment or answer an unexpected request.
2. Reach the organization through a website, app or phone number you already know or find on your own.
3. Use a different password for each account and turn on multi-factor authentication where it is offered. Never give a one-time code to someone who contacted you first.
4. Report the message with the service's spam or phishing button if it has one, then delete it.
5. If you typed your details into a suspicious page, change the password through the real service right away and review the account's recent activity.

No single visual clue proves a message safe or malicious. Contacting the organization independently, after a pause, is a stronger test than judging the wording.
