Someone posts a photo of a laptop on a sunny terrace, with a caption about finally quitting the nine-to-five and a link in the bio. Honest offers carry risk too. They take time, they cost money, and nobody can promise how they turn out. A pitch that hides all of that behind “easy money,” a guaranteed income or a deadline deserves a hard look before you put anything in.

## Questions to put to the offer

1. **The earnings claim.** Ask what it rests on, which costs it leaves out, and whether typical results are documented anywhere.
2. **Where the income comes from.** If the main way to earn is recruiting people or selling access, rather than serving ordinary customers, stop and look closer.
3. **Fees up front or on repeat.** Training, stock, coaching, subscriptions and “activation” fees all change what the offer really costs.
4. **A deadline for deciding.** A sound opportunity can be reviewed before you pay or sign.
5. **Fuzzy details about the work or product.** You should be able to say what is being sold, to whom, and how the pay is worked out.

## An invented post, not a real case

A social-media post says a paid coaching group will let you leave your job in a few weeks. It wants a fee today, says the real reward comes from building a “team,” and stays vague about what the product is and what it all costs. Testimonials and lifestyle photos prove nothing about typical results. Ask for the terms in writing, the total cost and the basis for the earnings claim, and walk away if nobody can provide them.

## A slower way to decide

1. Take your time before you pay, sign or invite anyone else in.
2. Search for the company or program name on your own, along with “review,” “complaint” and “scam.”
3. Add up every cost, including recurring charges and the hours you would have to put in.
4. Ask whether the pay comes from sales to ordinary customers rather than from recruiting.
5. Talk it over with someone who gains nothing from your decision.

For investments, follow the regulator and reporting rules that apply where you live. This guide is general education, not financial advice.
