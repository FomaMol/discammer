A pyramid scheme relies primarily on money from new participants, rather than demonstrated sales to ordinary customers. A Ponzi scheme pays earlier investors with money from later investors. Both can appear convincing at first because early payments or polished marketing can be used as social proof.

## Signs to examine

1. **Recruitment is the main route to compensation.** Be cautious when rewards depend more on bringing in people than on sales outside the program.
2. **High returns with little or no risk.** Every investment has risk; “guaranteed” returns deserve independent verification.
3. **No clear, verifiable revenue.** Ask what the product or service is, who buys it, and how income is generated.
4. **Complex fees or commissions.** Do not join a program whose payment structure you cannot explain in plain language.
5. **Pressure to buy inventory, training, or a place in the scheme.** A required buy-in can be part of the risk, not proof of an opportunity.

## Illustrative scenario — not a case report

An online group promises fast returns and shows screenshots of payouts. To participate, you pay an entry fee and recruit several people; no one can show independently audited sales to customers outside the group. Treat the screenshots as marketing, not verification. Do not transfer funds until you have checked registration, terms, compensation, and the business model through independent sources.

## Before you invest

1. Take time; do not let a limited-time offer decide for you.
2. Check the business and any investment professional with the relevant regulator in your jurisdiction.
3. Ask for written information about risks, fees, revenue, and how compensation is calculated.
4. Look for evidence of genuine sales to people outside the recruitment network.
5. Get an independent opinion before investing money you cannot afford to lose.

This guide explains common warning signs, not whether a particular company is lawful or safe. Investment rules and reporting routes vary by country.
