> peak window: 19:00-23:00 local time on weekdays; spikes after payroll dates.
> ~6% of victims contact law enforcement; most report only to the impersonated bank.
> link domains rotate every 48-96h; SMS sender IDs reuse the same 12-character pool.
> recommend: targeted briefings via /journal — see SMS scam newsletter.{% elif guide.slug == 'nigerian-letters' %}> classic 419 variant — still active 30+ years after first documented case in 1992.
> modern channels: Telegram DMs, LinkedIn InMail, dating apps, not the legacy email vector.
> average ask escalates from $200 "courier fee" to $5,000+ across 4-6 message exchanges.
> attackers operate primarily from West Africa and Eastern Europe; payouts route through crypto.
> recommend: never wire money to unlock a larger sum — that is the entire scam, by definition.{% elif guide.slug == 'investment-pyramids' %}> structure: returns paid from new-recruit deposits, not from any underlying investment.
> collapse window typically 9-18 months from launch; last 30 days produce ~40% of victims.
> promised returns above 20%/month should be treated as a confirmed pyramid signal.
> social-proof tactics: paid testimonials, fabricated "auditor" PDFs, staged office tours.
> recommend: verify the operator with the local financial regulator before any deposit.{% elif guide.slug == 'fake-online-stores' %}> storefronts copy real-brand product pages pixel-for-pixel; prices set 35-60% below RRP.
> domain age usually under 90 days; WHOIS data redacted or registered to a shell address.
> payment forms accept card-only — no PayPal, no cash-on-delivery — which is the tell.
> products never ship; chargeback windows close before victims realize the order is fake.
> recommend: search "{domain} reviews scam" and check the site age before ordering.{% else %}> active investigation — pattern indicators logged from open-source intelligence feeds.
