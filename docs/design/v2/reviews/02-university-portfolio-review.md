# Portfolio review — DiScammer as an admissions portfolio piece

**Reviewer role:** Admissions reviewer for a U.S. university program (Human-Computer Interaction /
Design / Computer Science with a design emphasis). This is the kind of written evaluation an
applicant's portfolio piece would receive when a committee asks "does this belong in the file, and
what does it demonstrate about the candidate?"

**Piece reviewed:** DiScammer — a multilingual anti-fraud education platform, second design iteration
(`DiScammer v2.dc.html`) plus its design-system documentation, design critique, and engineering
handoff.

**Recommendation:** **Strong portfolio anchor — lead with this piece.** It demonstrates the three
things committees actually look for (problem framing, process, and craft) and avoids the most common
student-portfolio failure modes.

---

## Why this works as a portfolio piece

### 1. It solves a real human problem, not a made-up one
Fraud is a concrete, high-stakes, widely-relatable problem. The framing — "build a working detector
in your own head," "install the pause" — shows the applicant can translate a fuzzy human need into a
crisp, defensible design goal. Admissions committees see a hundred "redesign Spotify" projects; a
multilingual anti-fraud tool aimed at non-technical, sometimes-scared adults stands out and signals
*intent*, not just exercise.

### 2. It shows process, not just a pretty screen
The file set tells a story a committee can follow:
- **v1 → v2 iteration** with a written rationale for every change. Showing your second iteration, and
  *why*, is more valuable than a single polished comp. It demonstrates the candidate can take critique
  and re-decide.
- **A documented design system** ("Field Office") with tokens, a typographic rule, a component
  inventory, and motion tiers. This signals systems thinking — the difference between decorating
  screens and building a language.
- **A self-authored design critique** that lists blockers and trade-offs. Self-critical maturity is
  exactly what graduate design programs screen for.

### 3. It demonstrates range: design AND engineering
The piece is a working artifact, not a static mockup. The live message-checker and link-inspector run
real client-side logic; the animations are GPU-only and respect reduced-motion. A candidate who can
both make the design decisions *and* implement them, with accessibility and performance constraints
honored, reads as unusually capable for an applicant pool.

### 4. It takes accessibility and ethics seriously
WCAG AA contrast, keyboard paths, reduced-motion, color-never-the-only-signal, and a privacy-first
stance ("nothing leaves your browser") are baked in, not bolted on. For HCI programs especially, an
applicant who treats accessibility and user trust as first-order design constraints is demonstrating
the field's core values, not just its aesthetics.

### 5. It handles internationalization thoughtfully
Russian-first / English-equal, with layout that breathes for Cyrillic and copy that is authored rather
than machine-translated, shows cross-cultural design sensitivity — a genuine differentiator and a sign
the candidate designs for real, specific users rather than a generic "user."

## What it demonstrates about the candidate (the committee's checklist)

| Committee looks for | Evidence in this piece |
|---|---|
| Problem framing | Reframes "anti-fraud course" as "install one reflex"; success = the user hesitates correctly |
| Systems thinking | A documented, tokenized design system with rules (two-register type), not one-off screens |
| Iteration & critique | v1→v2 with rationale; a self-authored senior-level design crit naming its own blockers |
| Craft | Restrained palette, deliberate typography, tasteful motion, real micro-interactions |
| Technical ability | Working client-side tools, performance-aware animation, semantic accessible markup |
| Values | Accessibility, privacy, ethics, and i18n treated as constraints, not afterthoughts |
| Communication | Clear written documentation a reviewer (or a developer) can actually follow |

---

## How the applicant should present it

1. **Lead with the problem and the one-line insight** ("the feature is the pause"), not the visuals.
2. **Show the v1→v2 diff explicitly.** A side-by-side of "brochure you look at" vs "tool you use"
   makes the iteration legible in seconds.
3. **Include one process spread** from the design system (the two-register type rule is the most
   teachable single idea).
4. **Demo the live tool** in the interview if possible — paste a scam message and let it light up.
   Interactive beats static every time.
5. **Name the constraints you honored** (AA, reduced-motion, RU/EN, privacy). Committees reward
   candidates who design *within* real constraints.

---

## Honest caveats (so the applicant isn't blindsided)

- Be ready to articulate **what is yours vs. tooling-assisted.** Committees increasingly ask. The
  honest, strong answer here: the *decisions* — problem framing, the system, the iteration rationale,
  the trade-offs — are the portfolio content; the implementation is the proof you can ship them.
- Have **one metric or user story** ready, even a small usability test with three people, to move from
  "looks considered" to "validated with users." That single addition would take this from strong to
  exceptional.
- Don't over-claim production status. Present it as a rigorous, working *design exploration with
  implementation* — which is exactly what it is, and exactly what's appropriate for a portfolio.

---

## Bottom line

This is the kind of piece an admissions committee remembers: a real problem, a clear point of view, a
documented system, an honest second iteration, and working, accessible, multilingual implementation.
It demonstrates that the candidate can think in systems, design with restraint, build what they
design, and hold ethics and accessibility as core. **Lead the portfolio with it**, add a small dose of
user validation, and it argues convincingly for admission.

— *Portfolio evaluation, U.S. university design/HCI admissions perspective*
